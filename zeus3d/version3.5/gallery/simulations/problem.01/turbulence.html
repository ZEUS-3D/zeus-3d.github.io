Simulation Gallery: Super-Alfvénic turbulence

Super-Alfvénic turbulence (vrms/arms>1) is a difficult task for even the most sophisticated MHD algorithms. Padoan & Nordlund (1999) successfully simulate super-Alfvénic turbulence with a staggered-mesh scheme.  Collins & Norman (pr. comm., 2008) and Klein (pr. comm., 2008) find mixed results with zone-centred Godunov schemes, where split schemes seem to have more success than unsplit schemes. However, Stone (pr. comm., 2009) claims his unsplit ATHENA code can do super-Alfvénic turbulence without problem, though this is questioned by Klein (pr. comm., 2009).  Regardless, the paucity of literature on this subject at the time of this writing is an indicator of its complexity and controversy.

The Consistent Method of Characteristics (CMoC) algorithm in ZEUS-3D is capable of performing super-Alfvénic turbulence simulations; indeed it was designed for it.  Here, I compare the results of the three MoC-based schemes in ZEUS-3D (MoC, Stone & Norman, 1992, ApJS, 80, 791; HSMoC, Hawley & Stone, 1995, JCP, 205, 509; and CMoC, Clarke 1996, ApJ, 457, 291), demonstrating the numerical difficulties that can arise.  The cause of these difficulties and why CMoC works are discussed in the document What is planar splitting?

The images in the table are all line-of-sight integrations of the magnetic pressure (B2/2) at the final epochs of 643 simulations of isothermal, super-Alfvénic turbulence using the three MHD algorithms available in ZEUS-3D.  Initially,

r =1, vrms= 5, cs = 0.1, Bx = 10-7 (in units where m0 =1). Kinetic energy is added to maintain vrms= 1.

Turbulence is initialised and driven in specified ranges of the wave-number, k (box length/wavelength), with the minimum wavelength being 2 zones.  Thus, for a 643 simulation, 1<k<32. The column headings, low-, mid-, high-, and all-k correspond to 1<k<4, 3<k<4, 3<k<32, and 1<k<32.

The MoC is subject to an "explosive-field instability" which is entirely numerical in origin and can drive a b = 1010 flow to a b ~ 1 flow within a single time step and in a single zone. This can evacuate the zone enough to drive the Alfvén time step to zero, bringing the simualtion to a halt.  Only the low-k MoC simulation reached t =40.  The mid-k simulation stopped at t =20, and the high- and all-k MoC simulations stopped at t ~3.

HSMoC and CMoC do not suffer from this instability, and all simulations are run to t =40 (two sound-crossing times, 2ts).  The driving k ranges are apparent in the final images.  In the low-k simulations, only the longest wavelengths are driven, causing excessive organisation of the fluid including large voids.  Indeed, at 323 resolution, the fluid becomes organised entirely on a single plane!  The mid-k simulations remain filled, and the magnetic field is organised into "filaments", as has been documented elsewhere.  Finally, the high- and all-k simulations show the effects of driving turbulence at the smallest wavelengths, with very fine details appearing in the magnetic field structure. Click on any image for an animation.

While there may be little to distinguish the HSMoC and CMoC simulations in the images above, interesting differences in how the magnetic field grows in time can be found in the history files.  When the highest wavenumbers are driven, CMoC exhibits magnetic field "eruptions" over the course of the simulation which can raise the mean magnetic energy density two orders of magnitude higher than the corresponding HSMoC simulation in which the "eruptions" are absent. "Eruptions" are distinct from the MoC "explosions" in that they do not cause excessive decline in the local Alfvén speed, they happen over significant periods of time (many tens of time steps), and seem to have a physical basis.  In particular, they are associated with localities of strong and sustained shear layers that stretch, and thus strengthen, a weak magnetic field.  The shear layer is eventually disrupted but, in some cases, not before the field has grown by an order of magnitude or more.  These "eruptions" enrich the rest of the grid with enhanced magnetic field and continue until the overall field strength is sufficient to resist being stretched further.  Why they don't appear in HSMoC simulations remains a mystery, and may point to numerical problems in CMoC or physical shortcomings in HSMoC.  They are absent in both the low-k and mid-k CMoC simulations, and their prominence in high-k CMoC simulations drops as the resolution is increased.

The history file to t =160 shows, among other variables, the rms velocities.  The rms fluid speed, vrms, is initialised at 5 and quickly falls to 1 (where it is driven), the sound speed, crms, is constant at 0.1 (isothermal gas), while the rms Alfvén speed, arms, increases exponentially from t ~1 to ~120 until it reaches crms where magnetic growth saturates.  The magnetic energy increases by three orders of magnitude between 0 < t < 1 during the rapid decline of vrms, then by another ten orders of magnitude between 1 < t < 120 (6ts), growing 1.7 orders of magnitude per sound crossing time.  At t =120, growth halts and the total magnetic energy remains more or less constant through t =160.  Thus, the system remains supersonic and super-Alfvénic (M = MA = 10), and evolves from b = 2 x 1013 to ~ 2.

The Fourier spectra of the variables at the final epoch show a reasonable inertial regime (power law of -5/3) in 3<k<25, particularly for the density and kinetic energy.
